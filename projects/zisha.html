Yixing Zisha AR

Exploring pottery through form, clay and craft.

I designed a mobile AR experience around a Fanggu teapot, then studied how perceived authenticity, usefulness and flow relate to willingness to use it.

Beyond the prototype

How do people’s perceptions of an AR craft experience relate to their willingness to use it?

Participants were recruited online through social media, including Xiaohongshu and WeChat groups. They received the Android application, image target and instructions, and were asked to explore all three modules on their own phones before completing an online survey.

Of 425 returned questionnaires, 388 passed the reported quality checks. A 29-item, seven-point questionnaire measured seven constructs, combining the stimulus–organism–response framework, the Technology Acceptance Model and flow theory.

Research procedure: thesis §§3.6–3.7 and §4.2, pp. 41–51.

A remote prototype study, not an on-site museum trial

Participants experienced the application independently, without a fixed session length. The study measured self-reported perceptions and intentions, not observed museum behaviour or learning gains. 78.9% of the sample was aged 19–30.

What the study found

Nine of ten hypothesised paths were statistically supported. These are associations within a cross-sectional model, not causal effects of particular design features.

Authenticity was linked to both value and engagement

Perceived authenticity was positively associated with perceived usefulness (β = 0.541) and flow (β = 0.452), both p < .001. Here, authenticity includes visual credibility, material representation and cultural accuracy.

Thesis §4.4.2, Table 4-5: H5 and H4; construct definition in §4.1.

Usefulness and flow were linked to intention to use

The paths from perceived usefulness (β = 0.412) and flow (β = 0.407) to behavioural intention were both significant (p < .001). They capture perceived cultural value and absorption in the experience—not measured learning performance.

Thesis §4.4.2, Table 4-5: H8 and H10.

Ease of use was not a significant direct predictor of intention

The direct path to behavioural intention was not significant (β = 0.068, p = .232). Ease of use was positively associated with usefulness and flow. This does not mean usability is unimportant.

Thesis §§4.4.2–4.4.3, Table 4-5: H9, H6 and H7.

From findings to design directions

The thesis translates the analysis and theoretical discussion into five directions. They are proposals for future design, not improvements evaluated in a second study.

Organise for understanding
Layer overview, detail and comparison around the craft object, instead of maximising the amount of information on screen.
Make the response clear
Keep module choices visible, make changes easy to recognise and provide recovery guidance when image recognition fails.
Build cultural credibility
Attend to vessel proportions, differences between clay materials and the accuracy of text, narration and craft footage.
Support sustained exploration
Give each module a clear purpose and avoid interruptions between reading, comparing materials and watching the process.
Design for the exhibition setting
Consider target placement, lighting, short visits and the effort of holding a phone. These deployment considerations still need on-site evaluation.

Adapted from thesis §5.3, pp. 64–69. The prototype shown above is the studied version, not a later implementation of every recommendation.

Where the evidence stops

This is a single-case, cross-sectional, self-report study with a predominantly young sample. It does not establish causality, long-term engagement or learning outcomes. The research prototype also simplifies material rendering and tracking, and has not established robustness under real museum lighting, crowding or prolonged use.

Thesis §6.2, pp. 72–74.
